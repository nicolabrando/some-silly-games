How to play

Controls

  • Up throttle  ·  Down brake (and reverse once stopped)
  • Left / Right steer  ·  on a phone, use the on-screen pad
  • Arrow keys or W A S D — pick either in the menu. Left‑handers and anyone who would rather keep a hand on the mouse can swap.
  • Free practice just goes: no start lights and no jumped-start rule, because alone on an empty circuit there is no grid to reset and nobody to gain a place over.
  • Space, P or Esc pause — or the Pause button in the corner. Works any time: on the grid, mid‑race, in qualifying. The clock stops with you, so a pause costs you nothing — and the screen shows you where you stand while it is stopped.

What you hear

The engines are yours — one per driver at the keyboard — and around them the circuit now makes its own noise:

  • Kerbs rattle and grass rumbles, both louder the faster you are going over them. If you are hearing either, you are losing time.
  • The tyres squeal when the car starts to slide, and the squeal rises as the slide gets worse — it starts at exactly the point the game begins laying rubber on the road, so black marks and noise mean the same thing.
  • Contact is heard wherever it happens: quieter the further it is from the middle of your screen, and from the side it happened on. A shunt behind you is a shunt you hear.
  • A beep per red light, a different one at green, the chequered flag, the VSC coming out and going in, and a short chime when you beat your own best lap.

Sound in the menu turns the lot — engines, music and effects — down or off together.

The cars

Every car is a single‑seater drawn from directly above, and the four chassis are meant to be told apart at a glance without reading the timing tower:

  • Aero is mostly wing — the widest front wing of the four, in two elements, bargeboards ahead of the sidepods and winglets on top of them, and a hard coke‑bottle waist.
  • Bolt is built to go straight: stub wings, no bargeboards, a long shark fin down the engine cover, and huge rear tyres.
  • Ridge is built to last: the widest, boxiest sidepods with cooling louvres cut into them, a big roll hoop and a blunter nose.
  • Torque is a dragster in a single‑seater's clothes: the biggest airbox in the field, narrow front tyres and the fattest rears in the game — the only way it puts that engine on the road.

The coloured band on the inner sidewall of each tyre is that car's compound, in the same colours as the timing tower — so you can see what the car in front of you is on without looking away from the road. In the tower, the small bar beside each compound pip is how much of that tyre is left — green, then amber under half, red under a quarter. A rival whose bar has gone red is a rival about to slow down.

Between the tyre choice and the lights, the grid recap shows the whole field at a glance: everyone's car, the compound and time their grid slot was earned on, and — the column worth reading — the compound each of them starts the race on. The countdown waits until you press start.

The camera

The screen is a window on the circuit, not the whole circuit: the camera follows your car, magnified, looking a little way down the road in the direction you are travelling. That is what lets circuits be bigger than the screen. The circuits marked (XL) in the track list live in worlds two to four times the size of the old canvas: Spa 7.5 km and twenty-one corners, Marathon 7.5 km and eighteen, Suzuka 5.8 km in the game's first tall world, Lungolago 5.9 km around a lake, Riviera 5.1 km along the sea, Colossus 5.1 km and fifteen corners, Lotus 3.7 km with a chicane on every side. They are long because there is more circuit, not because the straights went on for ever — which is also what keeps them fair to every chassis.

Suzuka is the second figure of eight, after Crossover, and the second portrait of a real place: the hooking First Corner, the ESSES, Dunlop, the hairpin, 200R, Spoon, a long backstretch carried over the bridge — you pass under it on the way to the hairpin, and three corners later you are the traffic above — then the 130R sweep straight into the Casio chicane. The esses are the point: six deep alternating corners whose road swings 65 px either side while the straight line through them exists only over the kerbs, at full commitment — measured, the AI's racing line cannot reach it and slaloms instead, so the esses are the one place on the calendar where a committed human line simply does not exist for the opposition. Enter wide, clip every kerb, keep it flat.

Lungolago and Riviera are the water pair. Lungolago wraps a lake clockwise and for half the lap the road is the shore: the south run is a causeway with the lake past one wall and a lagoon past the other, and on the west climb the road walks out onto a headland and wraps its tip with water on three sides. Riviera answers it anticlockwise: a 1750 px sea-front — the longest straight in the game, open water the whole way down — into a heavy hairpin around a cove, an old town, and a marina full of moored boats with the road wrapped around the basin. One turns right on balance, the other left; the water decides nothing and watches everything.

Spa is the other portrait of a real place: La Source, the plunge and climb through Eau Rouge and Raidillon, the Kemmel straight, Les Combes, Bruxelles, Pouhon, Les Fagnes, Stavelot, Blanchimont and the Bus Stop, in that order and at roughly those proportions. It is a portrait rather than a survey — the corners are as tight as a car of this size can take, not as tight as the map — and it is the only circuit on the calendar that is not mirrored to balance the steering, because Spa turns right and a mirrored Spa would be somewhere else.

  • Camera zoom in the menu sets the magnification: Wide shows more road ahead, Close puts you nearer the kerbs. It applies immediately, even mid‑session.
  • The minimap in the top-right corner shows the whole circuit, a dot per car (yours is ringed in white, wrecks turn grey), and a thin rectangle marking exactly where the window is looking.
  • Spectating, the camera follows the leader — or click any driver in the timing tower to follow them instead.

Two players, one keyboard

Set Players to 2 and a second car joins the field. One of you drives on the arrow keys, the other on W A S D — whichever scheme Player 1 takes, Player 2 automatically gets the other, so you can never fight over a key. There is still no split screen: one camera frames both cars, zooming out as the gap between you grows and back in as you regroup — so a two‑player race is also a fight over where the camera looks.

  • Each driver picks their own compound, before qualifying and again before the race. You do not have to agree.
  • You qualify together, both on track at the same time, and the grid is set on the times you actually set. Whoever finishes their laps first parks and waits.
  • Both cars score championship points, so a season is a straight head‑to‑head with the AI in between.
  • Your own lap, position, speed, lap time and tyres are on your own card at the bottom of the screen, colour‑keyed to your car.
  • You each get your own engine note, pitched differently and panned to opposite sides — your car is the one on your side of the sound.

Driving the car

The car does not turn on rails. Two things stop it:

  • Understeer. Carry too much speed into a corner and the front washes out: you turn the wheel and the car keeps going straight towards the grass. The cure is to slow down before the corner, not in it.
  • Power oversteer. Squeeze the throttle hard at low speed while turning — a hairpin exit — and the rear steps out and the car spins further than you asked. Ease off, or catch it early.

Both are much easier to provoke in the wet. Black marks on the tarmac mean you are sliding, and sliding is slow.

Surfaces

  • Tarmac — full grip.
  • Kerbs — the red and white strips lining every corner. They cost 20% of your grip and rattle the car, but they are meant to be used: running a wheel over one to straighten a corner is quick. Grass is not.
  • Grass — a third of the grip and heavy drag, and it lines the straights. Running wide there costs you the lap.
  • Barriers — hitting one square costs real damage; brushing along it barely does.
  • Damage. Your car takes less than the AI's, and light contact costs you nothing at all. The AI drives to a computed line with perfect lookahead; you have four arrow keys. A hard enough shunt will still end your race, but one misjudged corner will not.
  • Rain — grip collapses. Corner speeds drop by 10–15%, and some drivers handle it far better than others.

Damage

The bar above each car is its condition. Racing contact is free: leaning on someone, banging wheels at the start, nudging the car ahead into a corner all cost nothing. Past about a third of top speed of closing, damage climbs with the square of the impact, so a real shunt can still end your race. Below 60% the car loses grip and power, at 0% it is out. The drivers race hard and will not simply move over.

Timing tower

Down the left: the order by three-letter driver code (YOU is you), and the gap in seconds to the car directly ahead. Anyone more than a lap behind shows the lap deficit instead — a time gap to a car you have already lapped means nothing.

Championship

A season across every circuit with F1 points — 25 for a win down to 1 for tenth — plus the two extras: 1 point per place gained from your grid slot, up to 5, and 1 point for the fastest lap of the race. The fastest lap only pays if you finish in the top ten: set it and then park it in the wall and it counts for nothing, which is why a car three laps down cannot go hunting for it.

At the end you get the full season grid: every driver, every race, wins and podiums highlighted, DNFs marked, an F against whoever set the fastest lap of each race and a ★ for a Grand Chelem.

Racing

  • Lights. Move before they go out and the grid is reset — everyone back to their slot, wheels stopped, lap counters wiped — and you take a time penalty at the flag. The hold before the green is random, and it is drawn from the same range at a restart as at the first start, so jumping on purpose to be given an easier start to read buys you nothing.
  • How big the penalty is. Not a fixed number: 5% of a lap here × √(laps ÷ 5), never under a second and never over five. Five seconds is Formula 1's figure and it does not travel: there it is 5.6% of a ninety-second lap in a race whose finishers are four seconds apart, so it costs about one place. Here a lap is eighteen seconds and the field finishes 0.85s apart, so the same five seconds cost three to four places — a false start would decide the race rather than punish it. The square root is there because the field does not spread out in proportion to the distance: over three laps it finishes within 10.5s, over twenty within 20.5s. In practice that means one second on most circuits over a short race, 1.8s over five laps of Marathon, 3.6s over twenty of them. Every further offence costs the same again, and the exact figure is on the banner and in the race log.
  • Slipstream. Tuck in behind a car and you get a tow: more power, less drag. It builds the closer you get, so use it down the straight and pull out late. The car ahead has to be moving to leave a hole in the air — one that is stopped, crawling or spun gives you nothing.
  • Blue flags. A small blue flag over a car means it is about to be lapped and must let the leader through. If it appears over you, you are a lap down.
  • Everyone's compound is on the sheet. In qualifying, the timing tower and the final classification both carry the tyre each driver set their time on, beside the time itself — because that is part of what the time means. Half a second is a lot between two drivers and nothing at all between a soft and a hard.
  • Qualifying. You qualify before every race. The session is three laps: a warm‑up lap from a standing start, then two flying laps — your best of the two counts. Every opponent runs the same three laps of their own, simulated with the same physics in clean air, and their times drop into the tower on the left as they come in. The classification is the starting grid. Whatever weather qualifying is run in, the race is run in too. You can press End Qualifying to stop early and take whatever you have set. The barriers cost the same as in the race — wreck the car and your session is over on whatever time you had. The car is rebuilt for the race, so you carry the grid slot over, not the damage.
  • Reverse Grid in Championship. Tick it and the championship is decided by the standings instead of by qualifying: the leader starts last, the backmarker starts on pole. The better your season is going, the harder the next race gets. Round 1 has no standings to invert, so that grid is drawn at random.
  • Grand Prix preview. Every championship round opens on a circuit card: a map, length, corner count by hand, an estimated lap time and top speed (measured by actually lapping the circuit), and the weekend's weather. Skip this Grand Prix sits you out: the rest of the field still races the full distance for real championship points, simulated at speed, and you are classified DNS with nothing scored. You still get the full result sheet afterwards.
  • Racecraft. A driver stuck behind someone builds into an attack: they brake later, commit harder and close right up. Once they are alongside they are free to outpace you — so a move that has been started can actually be finished.
  • VSC. If a car is destroyed, a yellow VSC banner appears and every engine is cut to 28% while a crane drives out, lifts the wreck and carries it behind the barriers. Full power returns the moment the track is clear.
  • Tyres. You choose a compound before qualifying and again before the race, and the two can differ. Softer rubber is quicker while it lasts and falls away sharply once it is gone; the hard barely changes all race. Over a full race distance the three are within a fraction of a percent of each other — what you are choosing is when you want your performance, not how much. A set lasts the same fraction of any race, so a soft covers twice as many laps in a ten-lap race as in a five-lap one. Sliding the car wears it faster. The bar in the corner shows what is left.
  • Every season is kept. The seasons, from the menu, holds every championship you have run — filed from its first Grand Prix, so the ones you walk away from are there too. Open one for the full standings with starts, wins, podiums, poles, fastest laps, retirements and best finish per driver, plus the round-by-round grid; the strip at the top adds it all up across your career. Download everything writes a .txt that is both the race log and a save: Load a file, on that screen or on the Race Log, reads it back on any machine and merges it with what is already there — the same file twice changes nothing. Older logs work too: a .txt written before any of this existed has no data block, so the report itself is read and the seasons are rebuilt from the classifications in it. Those are marked from a log, because a log never carried the drivers’ colours or how long the calendar was meant to be. Each season in the list has a × to throw it away (it asks once first), and one you left unfinished has a Resume that picks it up at the round you stopped at — whatever season was waiting in the menu banner goes back into the list and can be taken up again the same way, so nothing is lost by changing your mind. A season left unfinished before this screen existed can be picked up too: the calendar, the standings and the results are the real ones, but the opponents’ skill spread is re-rolled and that season’s rival is dropped, because a season saved back then was not carrying them. The Win rate column is the share of the races you started that you won: a Grand Prix you sat out is not one you failed to win.
  • The Hall of Fame. At the top of The drivers, built from the same archive read the other way round — by driver, across every season on file. The roll of champions lists each finished season with its champion in gold; the table under it is one career row per name — seasons, titles, starts, wins, podiums, poles, fastest laps, retirements, average finish and points — with yours highlighted. Only finished seasons count — here and in every other statistic: single races are never filed, and an abandoned season counts for nothing unless you resume and finish it. Average finish skips retirements and skipped rounds.
  • Nightmare season. On the Championship tab: instead of drawing the calendar out of the hat, it picks the circuits that have gone worst for you — ranked by average finishing position, with a retirement counted as one place worse than the biggest field, so the places that keep breaking your car count as the nightmares they are. Season length is still yours: the option changes which circuits, never how many. The line under the settings names them before you start. If you have not raced enough circuits yet, the ones you have never seen fill the calendar. The set comes from your record rather than from the seed, so the same seed will not reproduce it once your record has moved, and seasons run this way are marked nightmare in the history.
  • And every circuit keeps its own history. The circuits now carries what has happened there: each card says how many races you have started and how many you won, and opening one adds your full record — podiums, poles, fastest laps, Grand Chelems, retirements, best and worst finish, average finishing position, points per race, how it has gone in the wet, your best lap on that layout, and when you first and last raced there — plus a table of everyone who has raced there, so you can see who owns the place. All of it counted from the season history, so a circuit only has a record once a championship round has been run on it: single races are not filed.
  • What you did here last time. The tyre screen carries your own record book for that circuit: your best lap on each compound, dry and wet in separate columns, with the column for today's weather picked out and your quickest in green. Every button also shows your time on that compound in the weather you are about to drive in, or a dash if you have never tried it. The percentages beside each tyre are the model's opinion; those times are the stopwatch's, and the two do not always agree.
  • The AI choose too. Each driver picks from their own style: Senna and Verstappen almost always take the soft, Prost and Lauda almost always the hard. Longer races push the whole field onto harder rubber.
  • Puddles. In the wet, standing water collects on the racing line. Hit one at speed and the car aquaplanes: the steering all but stops working and you carry straight on until you are through it. At walking pace you keep most of your control, so lifting is a real option — and going round is usually quicker than going through.
  • Places gained. One championship point per position recovered from the grid, up to five, on top of the usual score. It is listed separately everywhere so you can see what you earned by overtaking rather than by qualifying.
  • Virtual Safety Car. While the VSC is out the order is frozen: each car meets an invisible wall that travels with the one in front and cannot get past it. That means anybody in front — including a car you are about to lap. When the crane is home the banner counts down three seconds with tenths, so the green flag never catches you mid‑corner. Recover the places after that.
  • The crane. A recovery vehicle on the edge of the circuit is solid. Hit it and it costs you exactly what a barrier would — brushing it is cheap, spearing it is not. Give it room.
  • Grand Chelem. Pole, the win, the fastest lap and leading every single lap of the race — all four in one Grand Prix. It is marked with a gold ★ beside your name in the final championship standings, and on the race itself in the season grid.
  • The finish. Once the leader takes the flag, everyone else is classified on their next crossing of the line. If you are still out there two of the leader's best laps later, you are marked DNF.

The HUD

Lap · position · speed, then the live lap timer: green while you are up on your best lap, red once you have lost it. L is your last lap, B your best, and ★ marks a new personal best.

Opponents

The ten drivers are not reskins of each other. Senna is untouchable in the rain, Verstappen brakes later than anyone and will not yield, Prost never makes a mistake, Vettel is devastating in clean air but fussier in traffic, Alonso is the one you cannot pass. Difficulty scales their pace and their patience; on Impossible they run the true limit of the car and make no mistakes at all.

Free Practice

Your chosen circuit and weather, no opponents, as many laps as you like. Every lap is timed; hit Stop Session in the HUD and you get the full list with the best one marked.

Race Log

Every session is recorded — lap times, flags, contact, retirements, safety car periods, the final classification. Open it from the menu, or download it as a text file.
